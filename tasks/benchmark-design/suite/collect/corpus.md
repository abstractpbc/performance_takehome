## story | The lighthouse keeper's ledger

Every evening for thirty-one years, Agnes wrote three lines in the ledger: the wind, the state of the sea, and the ships she had seen pass. The entries were short because the lamp needed tending, and because she had never believed that anyone would read them.

The ledger came to the museum after the light was automated. A young archivist named Callum was asked to catalogue it, and at first he found it dull. "Wind north-west, moderate. Sea choppy. Two trawlers, one steamer." Page after page of it.

But in the winter of the third year, the entries changed. Next to the ships, Agnes had begun to add names, not of vessels but of people. "The Morwen, Jack Tully's boat, home late." "The Heron, the Pascoe boys, safe." Callum realised she had known the fishermen by their lights, by the way each boat rounded the point.

He spent the next week cross-checking the names against the parish records. Most of the men had lived long lives. One entry, though, stopped him. "The Grace, no light seen. Watched until dawn." Below it, the following night, in a smaller hand: "Still watching."

There was no record of the Grace returning. There was also no record of it being lost. Callum sat with the ledger for a long time, and then he did something archivists are not supposed to do. He turned to the next blank page and wrote the date, the wind and the sea, and that he had been watching too.

## story | The bakery on Fennick Street

The bakery opened at five, but Ruth arrived at three, because bread does not care what time you would like to be awake. She liked the hour. The street was empty except for the street sweeper, who waved at her every morning as if they shared a secret.

For twenty years she had made the same six loaves. Then, one spring, a boy of about ten began standing outside the window before school, watching her shape the dough. He never came in. After a week she opened the door and asked if he wanted to try.

His name was Elias, and he was hopeless at first. His loaves came out flat, or burnt on one side, or both. But he came back every morning, and he asked questions she had never thought to ask herself. Why did the dough need to rest? Why did the oven need steam? Why did she always make exactly six?

She did not have a good answer to the last one. It had simply always been six. So, that summer, they made a seventh: a dark rye with fennel seeds that Elias had read about in a library book. It sold out by eight o'clock.

Years later, when Ruth retired, the bakery passed to a young man with flour on his sleeves and a habit of asking why. There were twelve loaves on the shelf by then. The rye was still the first to go.

## story | Signal at 4 a.m.

The radio telescope picked up the pattern at four in the morning, which is when all strange things happen at observatories, because that is when the only person awake is the most junior one.

Noor checked it three times. A narrow spike, repeating every 1.3 seconds, from a patch of sky that should have held nothing interesting. Pulsars repeat, but this one drifted slightly in frequency, back and forth, like a voice rising and falling.

She knew the rules. Nine times out of ten, a signal like this came from Earth: a microwave oven in the staff kitchen, a satellite, a faulty cable. She walked to the kitchen. The microwave was unplugged. She checked the satellite database. Nothing overhead. She swapped the cable and the spike was still there.

At six, the senior astronomer arrived, listened to her explanation without interrupting, and then asked one question: "Did you check the car park?"

It turned out a delivery van had been parked beside the dish all night with a faulty reversing sensor, chirping into the receiver at 1.3-second intervals, its pitch drifting as the battery cooled. Noor was mortified. The astronomer was delighted.

"Write it up," he said. "Everyone finds a van eventually. Most people don't check the microwave first." She did, and the note was pinned to the control room wall for years, under the heading: Things That Are Not Aliens.

## story | The map that was wrong on purpose

Old cartographers sometimes added a street that did not exist, a small invented lane, so that if a rival copied their map, they could prove it. Mapmakers called these trap streets. Lena had read about them as a student and had always found the idea charming.

She found less charm in it when she inherited her grandfather's map shop and discovered that he had put a trap street on every map he had ever drawn. They were all the same: a short road called Wren Lane, tucked between two real streets, in a different place on every map.

Customers rarely noticed. But one afternoon an elderly woman came in holding a map from 1968 and asked, quite seriously, how to get to Wren Lane. She had lived there, she said, as a girl.

Lena explained, gently, that Wren Lane was invented. The woman listened, then unfolded the map and pointed. In the spot where her grandfather had drawn the lane, there had once been a terrace of houses, demolished decades ago. The woman had grown up in one of them. The street had had no official name, and the residents had simply called it Wren Lane because of the birds that nested in the hedges.

That evening, Lena went through her grandfather's papers and found a photograph of the terrace, with a small boy standing in front of it. On the back he had written: Wren Lane, 1941. Home.

## story | Overdue

The library book was returned forty-two years late, wrapped in brown paper, with a note and a cheque for £187.50. The note said: "I worked out the fine at the old rate. Sorry. It was a good book."

The librarian, Mr Osei, had never seen anything like it. The book was a field guide to British moths, borrowed in 1983 by someone called J. Whitlow. The cheque was signed by the same name, in a shaky hand.

The library did not charge fines anymore; they had been abolished years earlier. Mr Osei could have sent the cheque back. Instead he looked up the address on the envelope and, on his day off, took the bus to a small village forty miles away.

J. Whitlow turned out to be Josephine, eighty-six years old, who explained that she had borrowed the book as a young teacher, used it to run a moth club for her pupils, and had simply never been able to part with it. Some of those pupils, she said proudly, had become scientists. One of them now studied insects for a living in Canada.

Mr Osei returned the cheque. He also brought the book back with him, re-stamped with a new due date three weeks away, and suggested that she might like to start the club again at the village hall. She did. By the autumn it had eleven members, and the book was overdue again.

## explainer | Why bread rises

Bread rises because of gas trapped in a stretchy network of proteins. When flour is mixed with water, two proteins in the wheat, glutenin and gliadin, link together to form gluten. Kneading lines these proteins up into long, elastic strands, which is why a well-kneaded dough can be stretched thin enough to let light through.

Yeast supplies the gas. These single-celled fungi feed on the sugars in the flour and release carbon dioxide and alcohol as by-products. The carbon dioxide forms tiny bubbles, and because the gluten network is both stretchy and fairly airtight, the bubbles expand instead of escaping. That is the rise.

Temperature matters a great deal. Yeast is most active somewhere around body temperature; colder dough rises slowly, which many bakers prefer because the longer fermentation develops more flavour. Too hot, and the yeast dies.

In the oven, the process speeds up briefly. The heat makes the gas expand rapidly and turns the alcohol and water to vapour, giving the loaf a final burst of growth known as oven spring. Then the proteins set and the starches gelatinise, fixing the structure in place.

Steam in the early minutes of baking keeps the crust soft long enough for the loaf to expand fully. Once the steam is removed, the surface dries and browns. Those brown colours and much of the flavour come from reactions between sugars and proteins at high temperature.

## explainer | How noise-cancelling headphones work

Noise-cancelling headphones use a simple idea: sound is a wave of pressure, and two waves can cancel each other if one is the exact opposite of the other. Where one wave pushes, the other pulls, and the result is close to silence.

Each ear cup contains one or more small microphones that listen to the sound around you. A processor analyses that sound and generates a new signal with the same shape but inverted. The speaker plays this inverted signal along with your music, so that the outside noise and its opposite meet in your ear and largely cancel out.

This works best on steady, low-pitched sounds, such as the hum of an aircraft engine or the rumble of a train. These sounds change slowly, so the processor has time to predict them. Sudden, high-pitched sounds, like a voice or a dog barking, change too quickly to cancel completely. That is why headphones also rely on passive isolation: well-sealed ear cushions physically block much of the high-frequency noise.

Timing is critical. If the cancelling signal arrives even slightly late, the waves no longer line up, and some noise gets through or is even made worse. Modern headphones place microphones both outside and inside the ear cup, so they can check how well the cancellation is working and correct it many times per second.

Some models also offer a transparency mode, which does the reverse: it plays the outside sound through the speakers so you can hear announcements without taking the headphones off.

## explainer | What a credit score measures

A credit score is a number that estimates how likely you are to repay borrowed money on time. Lenders use it to decide whether to offer you a loan or credit card and what interest rate to charge. The exact formulas are owned by the companies that produce them, but they generally draw on the same few kinds of information from your credit report.

The most important factor is usually payment history: whether you have paid past bills on time. A single missed payment can lower a score, and the effect fades slowly over the years that follow. The second is how much of your available credit you are using. Someone with a £5,000 limit who owes £4,500 looks riskier than someone with the same limit who owes £500, even if both always pay on time.

The length of your credit history also counts, because a long record gives lenders more evidence. Opening many new accounts in a short period can lower a score temporarily, since it can be a sign of financial strain. Finally, a mix of different kinds of credit, such as a card and a loan, can help a little.

Things that usually do not appear in a credit score include your income, your savings and your employer, although a lender may ask for them separately. Checking your own score does not lower it; only certain applications for new credit do.

## explainer | Why the sky is blue

Sunlight looks white, but it contains every colour of the visible spectrum. As it passes through the atmosphere, it collides with molecules of nitrogen and oxygen, which are much smaller than the wavelength of light. When light meets particles this small, it is scattered in all directions, and shorter wavelengths are scattered far more strongly than longer ones.

Blue light has a shorter wavelength than red, so it is scattered much more. When you look at any part of the sky away from the sun, you are seeing sunlight that has been bounced towards you by air molecules, and that light is dominated by blue.

Violet light has an even shorter wavelength, so you might expect the sky to look violet. There are a few reasons it doesn't. Sunlight contains less violet than blue to begin with, some violet is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and our eyes are much less sensitive to violet than to blue.

At sunrise and sunset, sunlight travels through far more atmosphere to reach you. By the time it arrives, most of the blue has been scattered away from your line of sight, leaving the reds and oranges. Dust, smoke and water droplets, which are larger than air molecules, scatter all colours more evenly, which is why a hazy sky looks paler and clouds look white or grey.

## explainer | How vaccines train the immune system

The immune system learns from experience. When it meets a germ for the first time, it takes several days to build a strong response, and during that time the infection can make you ill. Once it has fought off the germ, it keeps memory cells that recognise it, so a second encounter is usually dealt with quickly, often before you notice any symptoms.

A vaccine gives the immune system that first experience without the danger of the real disease. Depending on the type, it might contain a weakened or inactivated form of the germ, a harmless piece of it such as a surface protein, or instructions that let your own cells make that protein for a short time. In each case, the immune system sees something that looks like the germ and responds by making antibodies and memory cells.

The mild side effects some people feel after a vaccine, such as a sore arm, tiredness or a slight fever, are signs of this response at work. They usually pass within a day or two.

Some vaccines need more than one dose, or a booster years later, because the memory they create fades over time or because the germ itself changes. When enough people in a community are protected, a disease struggles to spread, which also helps protect those who cannot be vaccinated, such as very young babies.

## code | Rate limiter with a token bucket

```python
import time
from dataclasses import dataclass, field


@dataclass
class TokenBucket:
    """Allow bursts up to `capacity` requests, refilling at `rate` requests per second."""

    capacity: float
    rate: float
    tokens: float = field(init=False)
    updated: float = field(init=False)

    def __post_init__(self) -> None:
        self.tokens = self.capacity
        self.updated = time.monotonic()

    def _refill(self) -> None:
        now = time.monotonic()
        self.tokens = min(self.capacity, self.tokens + (now - self.updated) * self.rate)
        self.updated = now

    def try_acquire(self, cost: float = 1.0) -> bool:
        self._refill()
        if self.tokens >= cost:
            self.tokens -= cost
            return True
        return False

    def wait_time(self, cost: float = 1.0) -> float:
        """Seconds until `cost` tokens will be available."""
        self._refill()
        missing = cost - self.tokens
        return max(0.0, missing / self.rate)


buckets: dict[str, TokenBucket] = {}


def allow(client_id: str) -> bool:
    bucket = buckets.setdefault(client_id, TokenBucket(capacity=20, rate=5))
    return bucket.try_acquire()
```

The service calls `allow` before handling each request and returns HTTP 429 when it is false.

## code | Shell script for nightly backups

```bash
#!/usr/bin/env bash
set -euo pipefail

SOURCE="/srv/app/data"
DEST="/mnt/backup/app"
KEEP_DAYS=14
STAMP="$(date +%Y-%m-%d)"

mkdir -p "$DEST"

# Snapshot the database first so the copy is consistent.
pg_dump --format=custom --file="$DEST/db-$STAMP.dump" appdb

# Hard-link unchanged files against the previous run to save space.
LATEST="$(ls -1d "$DEST"/files-* 2>/dev/null | tail -n 1 || true)"
if [[ -n "$LATEST" ]]; then
  rsync -a --delete --link-dest="$LATEST" "$SOURCE/" "$DEST/files-$STAMP/"
else
  rsync -a --delete "$SOURCE/" "$DEST/files-$STAMP/"
fi

# Remove backups older than the retention window.
find "$DEST" -maxdepth 1 -name 'db-*.dump' -mtime +"$KEEP_DAYS" -delete
find "$DEST" -maxdepth 1 -type d -name 'files-*' -mtime +"$KEEP_DAYS" -exec rm -rf {} +

echo "backup $STAMP complete"
```

It runs from cron at 02:30 every night on the application server.

## code | React component for a search box

```tsx
import { useEffect, useState } from "react";

type Result = { id: string; title: string };

export function SearchBox({ onSelect }: { onSelect: (r: Result) => void }) {
  const [query, setQuery] = useState("");
  const [results, setResults] = useState<Result[]>([]);
  const [loading, setLoading] = useState(false);

  useEffect(() => {
    if (query.trim().length < 2) {
      setResults([]);
      return;
    }
    setLoading(true);
    fetch(`/api/search?q=${encodeURIComponent(query)}`)
      .then((r) => r.json())
      .then((data: Result[]) => setResults(data))
      .finally(() => setLoading(false));
  }, [query]);

  return (
    <div className="search">
      <input
        value={query}
        onChange={(e) => setQuery(e.target.value)}
        placeholder="Search products"
        aria-label="Search products"
      />
      {loading && <span className="spinner" />}
      <ul>
        {results.map((r) => (
          <li key={r.id} onClick={() => onSelect(r)}>
            {r.title}
          </li>
        ))}
      </ul>
    </div>
  );
}
```

## code | SQL for monthly active users

```sql
-- Monthly active users: anyone with at least one session in the calendar month.
WITH sessions_by_month AS (
    SELECT
        user_id,
        date_trunc('month', started_at) AS month
    FROM sessions
    WHERE started_at >= DATE '2025-01-01'
    GROUP BY user_id, date_trunc('month', started_at)
),
first_seen AS (
    SELECT user_id, MIN(month) AS first_month
    FROM sessions_by_month
    GROUP BY user_id
)
SELECT
    s.month,
    COUNT(*) AS active_users,
    COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE f.first_month = s.month) AS new_users,
    COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE f.first_month < s.month) AS returning_users
FROM sessions_by_month s
JOIN first_seen f USING (user_id)
GROUP BY s.month
ORDER BY s.month;
```

## policy | Remote work policy

Remote work must be done from within the country where the employee is employed, because working abroad can create tax and legal obligations for both the employee and the company. Short periods of work abroad, of up to ten working days a year, can be approved in advance by HR.

Equipment remains company property and must be returned on leaving.

Company data must only be accessed on company devices, and screens should not be visible to others in public places. Confidential calls should not be held in cafés, trains or other shared spaces.

Remote work is not a substitute for childcare or other caring responsibilities during working hours, although we support flexible hours where possible. Managers may review the arrangement if it is not working for the team, and will discuss any concerns with the employee before making changes.

## incident | Checkout outage, 12 June

Summary: between 14:02 and 14:49 UTC, about 38% of checkout attempts failed with an error page. Roughly 4,100 orders were affected. No payments were taken for failed attempts.

Timeline: at 13:55 a configuration change was deployed to the payment service, raising the connection pool size from 50 to 200. At 14:02, error rates began to climb. At 14:09, the on-call engineer was paged. Initial investigation focused on the payment provider, whose status page showed a minor incident in another region. At 14:31, an engineer noticed that the database was rejecting new connections because the total across all instances had exceeded its limit. The change was rolled back at 14:44, and error rates returned to normal by 14:49.

Root cause: the payment service runs twelve instances. The new pool size meant the service could open up to 2,400 connections, while the database accepts 1,000. The change had been tested in staging, which runs only two instances.

What went well: the rollback was quick once the cause was found, and no data was lost.

What went poorly: it took 29 minutes to find the cause, partly because the provider's status page was a distraction. The deploy did not show up in the incident channel automatically.

Actions: add a check that pool size times instance count stays under the database limit; post every deploy to the incident channel; make staging run at least four instances.

## incident | Data pipeline delay, 3 March

Summary: the daily sales report was delivered seven hours late on 3 March. The figures were correct, but several regional managers made stock decisions that morning using the previous day's numbers.

What happened: the pipeline that builds the report starts at 01:00 and normally finishes by 04:30. On 3 March, an upstream system that sends store transactions was moved to a new server the evening before. The new server's clock was set to local time instead of UTC, so its files were stamped one hour in the future. The pipeline treats files with future timestamps as incomplete and waits for them to "finish", retrying every fifteen minutes.

The pipeline did not fail; it simply kept waiting. Because there was no alert for a late report, only for a failed one, nobody was notified until a manager emailed the data team at 09:15. The clock was corrected at 10:40, and the report finished at 11:32.

Lessons: waiting without a time limit hid the problem. A report that is late should be treated with the same urgency as one that fails, because the business impact is similar.

Actions: alert if the report has not been delivered by 06:00; fail loudly if a file's timestamp is more than ten minutes in the future; include a check for clock settings in the server migration checklist; add a "data as of" timestamp to the top of the report, so readers can see when they are looking at old figures.

## docs | Getting started with the Lumen CLI

Lumen is a command-line tool for managing static websites. It builds your pages from Markdown, checks for broken links and publishes the result to any storage bucket.

Install it with your package manager, then create a new site in an empty folder:

```
lumen init my-site
cd my-site
lumen serve
```

`lumen serve` builds the site and opens it at http://localhost:4000. It rebuilds automatically whenever you save a file, so you can see your changes as you write.

Your pages live in the `content/` folder. Each Markdown file becomes a page, and folders become sections. Put a short block at the top of each file to set its title and date:

```
---
title: Hello world
date: 2025-05-01
---
```

Images and other files go in `static/` and are copied as they are. Layouts are in `layouts/` and use simple templates; you only need to change them if you want a custom design.

When you're ready to publish, set the bucket in `lumen.toml` and run `lumen deploy`. The deploy command only uploads files that have changed, and it refuses to run if `lumen check` finds broken links, unless you pass `--force`.

## docs | Lumen CLI: configuration reference

All settings live in `lumen.toml` at the root of your site. Every setting is optional unless noted.

`title` sets the site name, used in page titles and feeds. `base_url` is required for deploys and should be the full public address, including `https://`. Links in feeds and sitemaps are built from it, so a wrong value produces broken links that only show up after publishing.

`[build]` controls the output. `output_dir` defaults to `public`. `drafts = true` includes pages marked as drafts, which is useful for previews but should never be set on a production deploy. `minify` removes whitespace from HTML, CSS and JavaScript and is on by default.

`[deploy]` describes where the site goes. `bucket` is the storage bucket name, and `region` its location. `cache_control` sets the header sent with each file; the default caches pages for five minutes and static files for a year. If you change an image without changing its file name, readers may see the old one for a long time, so it's best to give changed images new names.

`[check]` controls link checking. `external = false` skips links to other websites, which makes checks much faster. `ignore` is a list of patterns for links that should never be checked, such as pages that require a login.

Settings can be overridden from the command line, for example `lumen build --set build.drafts=true`.

## docs | Lumen CLI: troubleshooting

**The site builds locally but pages are missing after deploy.** Check `base_url`. If it ends in a sub-path, such as `/docs`, every link must include it. Run `lumen check --external=false` against the built folder to find links that point to the wrong place.

**Deploys are slow.** Lumen compares files by their content, not their dates, so it should upload only what has changed. If every file uploads every time, something in your build is changing all pages, often a timestamp in a layout. Remove it, or set it from the page's own date.

**`lumen serve` doesn't pick up changes.** Some editors save files by writing a new file and renaming it, which can confuse the file watcher. Restarting `serve` fixes it; on Linux, raising the system limit on watched files may also help on large sites.

**Images look blurry.** Lumen resizes images to the widths listed in `[images] widths`. If the largest width is smaller than the space the image fills on screen, it will be stretched. Add a larger width, or use the original file for that image.

**Error: "front matter is not valid".** The block at the top of the file must start and end with three dashes, with nothing before the first set. A blank line or a stray character before it is the usual cause.

## recipe | Weeknight lentil soup

This soup takes about forty minutes, uses one pot, and tastes better the next day. It serves four.

Ingredients: 2 tablespoons olive oil, 1 onion, 2 carrots, 2 sticks of celery, 3 cloves of garlic, 1 teaspoon ground cumin, 1 teaspoon smoked paprika, 250 g red lentils, 1 tin of chopped tomatoes, 1.2 litres vegetable stock, juice of half a lemon, a handful of spinach, salt and pepper.

Chop the onion, carrots and celery into small pieces. Heat the oil in a large pan over a medium heat and cook the vegetables for about eight minutes, stirring now and then, until the onion is soft and starting to colour. Add the crushed garlic and spices and cook for one more minute, until fragrant.

Rinse the lentils in a sieve until the water runs clear, then add them to the pan with the tomatoes and stock. Bring to the boil, then lower the heat and simmer for twenty to twenty-five minutes, until the lentils have broken down. Stir from time to time, as lentils can stick to the bottom of the pan.

Stir in the spinach until it wilts, then add the lemon juice and season well. For a smoother soup, blend half of it and stir it back in. If it becomes too thick, add a little water. Serve with crusty bread, or a spoonful of yoghurt on top.

## recipe | Simple sourdough loaf

You will need an active starter that has been fed four to eight hours earlier and is bubbly.

Mix 375 g water with 100 g starter until cloudy, then add 500 g strong white flour and stir until no dry flour remains. Cover and leave for an hour. Add 10 g salt with a splash of water and squeeze it through the dough until it disappears.

Over the next two to three hours, do four sets of stretch and folds, about thirty minutes apart. With a wet hand, lift one side of the dough, stretch it up and fold it over, then turn the bowl and repeat on all four sides. The dough will become smoother and stronger each time.

Leave the dough to rise until it has grown by about half and has bubbles on the surface. How long this takes depends on the temperature; in a warm kitchen it may be three hours, in a cool one, six.

Tip it onto a lightly floured surface, shape it into a tight round and place it seam side up in a floured basket or bowl lined with a tea towel. Cover and refrigerate overnight.

The next day, heat the oven to 250 °C with a lidded pot inside. Turn the loaf onto baking paper, slash the top and lower it into the pot. Bake with the lid on for 20 minutes, then remove the lid, lower the heat to 230 °C and bake for a further 20 to 25 minutes, until deep brown. Let it cool for an hour before slicing.

## essay | In praise of boring technology

There is a particular kind of excitement that comes with a new tool. It promises to solve the problem you are struggling with, and the examples in its documentation always work. The trouble is that the examples are not your system. Your system has odd data, old dependencies and a team that will be maintaining it in five years, long after the excitement has faded.

Boring technology has one great advantage: its failures are known. When an established database misbehaves, someone has written about it, and there is probably a setting to fix it. When a new one misbehaves, you may be the first person to find out how.

This does not mean never adopting anything new. It means being honest about the cost. Every new tool needs to be learned, monitored, upgraded and explained to new team members. A team can only afford a small number of these at once. It is worth spending them on the problems that matter most to the business, rather than on whichever tool happens to be popular this year.

A useful question before adopting something is: what would we do if it stopped being maintained? If the answer is "rewrite a large part of our system", the tool is a much bigger commitment than it first appears.

None of this is an argument against curiosity. Try new things on side projects, in prototypes, in places where failure is cheap. Keep the core of the system boring, so that the interesting problems are the ones your users have, not the ones your tools create.

## essay | The case for writing things down

Many teams rely on memory more than they realise. Decisions are made in meetings, refined in chat threads and then held in the heads of the people who were there. This works until someone leaves, goes on holiday or simply forgets, and then the same discussion happens again, often with a different outcome.

Writing a decision down takes a few minutes. A short note saying what was decided, what the alternatives were and why they were rejected is enough. It doesn't need to be formal. What matters is that it exists and can be found.

The benefits go beyond memory. Writing forces clarity. It is easy to agree in a meeting with a vague idea; it is much harder to write it down without noticing the gaps. Many problems are caught at this stage, before any work has been done.

Written notes also make teams fairer. In meetings, the loudest voices tend to win. A written proposal gives everyone time to think, including people who are new, quiet or working in another time zone. Comments can be weighed on their merits rather than on who made them.

There is a cost: notes go out of date, and out-of-date notes can mislead. The answer is not to write less, but to date everything and mark what has been replaced. A note that says "superseded, see the newer decision" is still useful. It tells the next person that the question was asked, and where the answer now lives.

## review | Review: the Tern 2 e-reader

The Tern 2 is the best e-reader I have used for reading books, and one of the more frustrating for anything else.

The screen is the highlight. Text is sharp, the front light is even across the whole page and the warm-light setting makes reading at night genuinely comfortable. The page turn is fast enough that I stopped noticing it after a day. Physical page-turn buttons, which many rivals have dropped, make one-handed reading easy.

Battery life is excellent. With the light at about half brightness and Wi-Fi off, I read for close to five weeks before needing to charge it. The device is light and the textured back is easy to grip, although the case sold separately is overpriced.

The software is where it falls down. Importing books from anywhere but the official store is fiddly; the device supports common formats, but only after you install a desktop app that is slow and asks for an account. Notes and highlights can be exported, but only by email, one book at a time. The built-in dictionary is good, but there's no way to add others.

Overall, if you buy most of your books from the official store and simply want to read, the Tern 2 is easy to recommend. If you have a large library from elsewhere, or you take a lot of notes, look at the alternatives first.

## review | Review: Harbour Kitchen

Harbour Kitchen opened in the old customs house in the spring, and it is already hard to get a table on a Friday night. Having eaten there twice, I can see why, though it isn't without flaws.

The menu is short and changes often, built around fish landed that morning. On my first visit, a plate of grilled mackerel with pickled rhubarb was the best thing I've eaten this year: sharp, smoky and generous. A crab tart was rich without being heavy. On the second visit, the hake was perfectly cooked, but the sauce was so salty that it was hard to finish.

The room is beautiful, with high ceilings and big windows over the harbour, but it is loud. Once it fills up, conversation becomes difficult, and the music doesn't help. If you want a quiet dinner, ask for one of the booths at the back or go early in the week.

Service was friendly and quick on both visits, and the staff clearly know the menu well; when I asked about the wine, our server suggested something cheaper than the one I had picked, which I appreciated.

Prices are fair for the quality, if not cheap. Two courses and a glass of wine came to about £45 a head. Book ahead, avoid the busiest nights if noise bothers you, and order whatever the mackerel is doing that day.
